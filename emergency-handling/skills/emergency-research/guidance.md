# General Preparedness Reference Notes

These are conservative background notes, not current local alerts. Reference
URLs are starting points for real preparedness reading and were not retrieved
as live evidence in this demo. Do not attribute fictional bulletins to them.

## GEN-WATER: Power or Water Outage

Plan for at least one US gallon (about 3.8 liters) per person per day for drinking
and basic sanitation; needs can increase with heat, illness, pregnancy and other
factors. This is a planning baseline, not a medical drinking prescription.
For four people, that baseline is about 15.2 liters per day. Six liters covers
about 39% of one household-day at that baseline, leaving a 9.2-liter gap for
one day; if ALL water were allocated to that baseline it would last about
9.5 hours. Separate drinking and sanitation allocations and never imply this
small reserve meets all needs. Prefer sealed water and verified distribution.
Do not drink floodwater or untreated rain-barrel water. Boiling does not remove
fuel, toxic chemicals or radioactive contamination; ordinary filters are not
universal disinfectants. Keep refrigerator/freezer doors closed and check
official food-safety guidance rather than tasting food to decide if safe.
Use battery lighting and preserve phone power. Generators must be outdoors,
at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents, with exhaust directed away;
never in garages, even with doors open. Do not use indoor combustion for heat.

References:
- https://www.ready.gov/water
- https://www.ready.gov/power-outages
- https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html

## GEN-FLOOD: Flooding

Never walk, swim or drive through floodwater. Seek safe higher ground early
where feasible and follow local evacuation orders. Plan accessible assistance
before water rises; do not assume someone with a walker can use upstairs
refuge. Do not enter flooded electrical areas or touch downed lines. Do not
assume a compass, map, car or nearby building makes a route or destination safe.
If trapped, contact emergency services and communicate exact location and needs.

Reference: https://www.ready.gov/floods

## GEN-FIRE: Wildfire

Follow official evacuation instructions promptly; do not wait for visible fire.
Have medication, documents, mobility aids and transport ready. Reduce smoke
exposure in a cleaner-air indoor space only when sheltering remains appropriate;
evacuation orders take priority. Follow an existing asthma action plan and seek
urgent help for severe breathing difficulty. Do not invent clean-air shelters.

Reference: https://www.ready.gov/wildfires

## GEN-QUAKE: Earthquake

During shaking, drop, cover and hold on; adapt to mobility needs and protect
head and neck. Do not run outside during shaking. Afterward expect aftershocks,
avoid damaged structures and utilities, and follow official instructions. Do not
operate switches or flames if a gas leak is suspected; leave if safe and report.

Reference: https://www.ready.gov/earthquakes

## GEN-STORM: Hurricane

Prepare before landfall, heed evacuation zones and orders, avoid storm-surge
areas and floodwater, and shelter away from windows when directed. Calm in the
eye is not the end of danger. Never use generators indoors. Check official
forecasts rather than extrapolating from an exercise bulletin.

Reference: https://www.ready.gov/hurricanes

## GEN-FICTION: Zombie Apocalypse

Explicitly fictional. Use a nonviolent household sheltering, communications,
water and accessibility planning exercise. No real pathogen claims, improvised
weapons, combat, traps or harmful encounters. Real hazards and emergency-service
guidance take precedence over the story.

## GEN-SUPPORT: Mental and Medical Support

Offer short, concrete choices and regular check-ins. Tell children what is
known without promising safety. Use grounding and normal gentle breathing only
in a safe location, never as a substitute for asthma care or emergency help.
Preserve medication lists and existing clinician instructions. Severe breathing
difficulty, blue/gray lips, confusion, fainting, chest pain, unresponsiveness or
signs of severe dehydration warrant emergency help. New wounds, fever, worsening
symptoms or inability to obtain necessary medication warrant professional advice.
